# Findings that are not about models

Things this benchmark turned up that are worth recording separately: measurement
defects that produced published numbers, and cost results that contradict the
reason people reach for these models.

## The bug that produced two published results

Both TabNet and FT-Transformer were characterised in earlier notes on numbers
generated by a run in which they were largely not training.

The wrappers split off a validation set for early stopping with
`rng.permutation` seeded from a constant, unstratified. On any dataset where a class
missed that split, the logloss metric raised — and because the seed was fixed, it
raised on *every* trial, *every* fold, *every* time.

The failure was silent, and worse than silent: a dataset whose every fit raises
completes **fast**. Optuna caught the exception, marked the trial failed, and moved
on in milliseconds. So the dataset finished quickly, recorded a NaN or a score from
whatever partial folds survived, and the run looked healthy.

The damage:

| model | datasets with no score | what the published numbers meant |
|---|---|---|
| TabNet | 26 of 146 | cost measured on mostly-failing fits |
| FT-Transformer | 79 of 146 | mean over the 67 that happened to work |

The clearest single case is `abalone-7class`: the log records 1736.9 s of TabNet
time with **zero** completed fits — no early-stopping line, no max-epochs line. That
1736.9 s is pure wrapper overhead for 324 fits that each raised on entry.

Consequence for TabNet's headline number: its published 107.3 h total made it look
like a cheap model that simply performs badly. Measured with every fit training, it
costs 485.3 h, the most of any model, and still ranks last. TabNet is not a cheap
weak model; it is an expensive weak one.

**The lesson is about coverage, not about these two models.** A mean is only
meaningful next to the count it was taken over. Check the all-NaN count before
trusting any aggregate — the run that produces the worst numbers is the one that
looks fastest.

## A default batch size trained TabNet on nothing

With the split fixed and 144 of 146 datasets scored, TabNet still averaged 0.5946 —
last by 0.18, ahead of CatBoost on 2 of 144 datasets. The coverage check above passed.

`batch_size` was fixed at 1024 and never tuned, and pytorch-tabnet's `fit` defaults
to `drop_last=True`. An inner fit trains on about 48 % of a dataset, so below roughly
2 100 rows its only batch is incomplete and gets dropped: **zero gradient steps**.
Early stopping then fires on the untrained model, the fit returns in under a second,
and the score lands next to the class-prevalence baseline. That was 82 of 146
datasets.

| dataset | prevalence baseline | broken | fixed | CatBoost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `connectionist-vowel` | 0.0909 | 0.1280 | 0.9853 | 0.9967 |
| `cnae-9` | 0.1111 | 0.1567 | 0.9419 | 0.9800 |
| `semeion` | 0.1000 | 0.1406 | 0.9113 | 0.9877 |
| `dermatology` | 0.2012 | 0.3070 | 0.9903 | 0.9932 |

Below 1024 training rows the batch is now an eighth of the split, and only a
single-row remainder is dropped, since BatchNorm cannot take one. On the 82 affected
datasets TabNet moves from 0.4662 to 0.7345 (CatBoost 0.8172 on the 80 of them it
runs on); overall from 0.5946 to 0.7507, and from 2 to 11 datasets ahead of the
uniform-start CatBoost, 7 ahead of the current one. It still ranks last.

**A full count of scores is not enough either.** An untrained classifier returns a
valid number on every dataset. The tell is a score sitting on the prevalence
baseline, which `scripts/check_prevalence_baseline.py` now computes for every stored
pair — see the next section.

## The floor this metric starts from

ROC AUC begins at 0.5 no matter what the data looks like, so a useless model is
obvious from the number alone. Weighted PR AUC begins at the class prevalence: the
positive-class share for binary, the sum of squared class shares for multiclass.
That floor ranges from 0.01 to 0.95 across these 146 datasets, so a score means
nothing without it. `benchmark.metrics.pr_auc_baseline` computes it, and a test
pins it to what a constant predictor actually scores.

`scripts/check_prevalence_baseline.py` compares every stored score against its own
floor. Nine of roughly 2 300 pairs sit within 0.01 of it, seven of them on two
datasets worth knowing about:

| dataset | baseline | flagged | best model | models above the floor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `thoracic-surgery` | 0.8511 | TabNet 0.8376, SVC 0.8515 | TabFM 0.9132 | 15 / 16 |
| `planning-relax` | 0.2857 | five, from LightGBM 0.2478 up | SVC 0.4044 | 11 / 16 |

TabNet's 0.8376 on `thoracic-surgery` is the illustration: in a column of means it
reads as one of the better results in the benchmark, and it is below what predicting
the majority class achieves. The flagged pairs are not datasets that defeat
everything — most models clear the floor on both — which is what makes them worth
looking at.

The check prints a report and is not a test that fails. A dataset can legitimately
defeat a model, and a build that breaks on an honest result gets switched off.

## Label ordering silently changed the metric

`pr_auc_score` reads `y_prob[:, 1]` on binary problems, so which class counts as
positive follows label ordering. The classical and foundation paths encoded labels
differently, and on the affected binary datasets the two families were scored
against opposite classes.

This did not produce obviously wrong numbers. It produced *interesting* ones, which
is worse. It generated an entire published finding: a table of 13 datasets where
foundation models supposedly collapsed, and two successive explanations for it —
first "severe class imbalance", then a correction to "small binary data". Both
described an artefact.

| dataset | published gap | after the fix |
|---|---|---|
| `blood-transfusion-service` | +0.3661 | −0.0122 (foundation wins) |
| `appendicitis` | +0.2361 | −0.0314 (foundation wins) |
| `seismic-bumps` (TabICL vs CatBoost) | +0.73 | +0.0026 |
| `thoracic-surgery` (TabICL vs CatBoost) | +0.66 | +0.0002 |

The last column is as measured at the time, against the uniform-start CatBoost and
with TabPFN 2.6 among the foundation models. With the current models, 3 of 146
datasets have any classical model ahead of every foundation model by more than 0.02.

A finding that is large, clean, and explainable deserves more suspicion than a
messy one, not less. Both explanations fit the bad data comfortably.

## AutoML rows were joined by position

The original AutoML runs covered 142 of the 146 datasets. The figure notebooks sliced
`evaluated_datasets` positionally, which assumes the four missing datasets are last.
They are not — the gaps fall at indices 61, 62, 68 and 69 (`movement-libras-10`,
`movement-libras`, `ozone-eighthr`, `ozone-onehr`).

**81 of 142 rows were attributed to the wrong dataset.** Recovered by DP sequence
alignment against the score vectors: the aligned mapping costs 12.797 / 12.729 with
correlation 0.80 between the two frameworks, against 19.889 / 19.798 and correlation
0.37 for the positional assumption. The names are now written into the joblib files
and joined by name.

The corrected numbers moved AutoML from "comparable to individual gradient boosters"
to the top of the benchmark by a clear margin. That margin was itself an artefact,
covered next.

## The published AutoML numbers were ROC AUC

The 300 s AutoGluon and MLJAR results published until September 2026 were ROC AUC,
while every other model in the benchmark is scored by weighted PR AUC. On imbalanced
and multiclass data ROC AUC runs well above PR AUC, so both frameworks came out on
top.

They were never run in this fork. They are the upstream project's results from
2020-21 — every fold-score row of the published files, 137 for AutoGluon and 139
for MLJAR, matches `03_autogluon_sec_300.pickle` and `04_mljar_sec_300.pickle`
exactly — carried over when the benchmark was refactored and switched its metric to
PR AUC. The upstream scripts scored with `roc_auc_score`, fitted AutoGluon on
`roc_auc` and MLJAR on `auc`, and used the library versions of that time.

It surfaced when the frameworks were re-run to store probabilities for the
calibration figure, and the new PR AUC came out 0.054 below the published number
for AutoGluon. Scoring the stored probabilities both ways shows which metric the old
files hold. Over the 127 datasets both runs share:

| framework | published vs new PR AUC | published vs new ROC AUC |
|---|---|---|
| AutoGluon | median \|diff\| 0.0338 | median \|diff\| 0.0030 |
| MLJAR | median \|diff\| 0.0410 | median \|diff\| 0.0031 |

The largest drops are on multiclass and imbalanced datasets, where the two metrics
diverge most: `abalone-11class` fell from 0.764 to 0.279, and `bank-marketing` from
0.9161 to 0.5740 while its re-run ROC AUC is 0.9175. The 1000 s MLJAR file was run in
this fork and is PR AUC, a median 0.009 from the new 300 s run.

The September re-run used AutoGluon 1.6.3 and MLJAR 1.3.2, now pinned, with
`config.N_JOBS = 16` on a 14-core machine, and took 43.1 and 37.4 hours over 131
datasets. For most of the MLJAR run an orphaned analysis process held one core; a
deliberate test with six competing processes showed no significant shift in MLJAR's
scores (p = 0.20).

Scored like everything else, AutoGluon is below every foundation model and above
every classical model: 0.8427 against CatBoost's 0.8342 over the same 131 datasets,
0.8464 after the re-run in the next section.
MLJAR ties CatBoost. What the September re-run adds is
calibration: both frameworks now appear in the calibration table, near the
foundation models rather than the classical ones.

## AutoGluon re-run with nothing excluded and Ray

The published 300 s run departed from AutoGluon's defaults in three ways.
`excluded_model_types` named classes rather than registry keys, so AutoGluon ignored
it and trained its neural networks anyway; FastAI was absent only because `fastai`
was not installed; and Ray was off, because it hung under AutoGluon 1.5.0, so bagging
folds trained one after another, on 2-5 of the 14 cores when watched. The re-run
installs `fastai`, drops the argument and adds Ray 2.58.0, on AutoGluon 1.6.3 with the
same 300 s per fold and `num_cpus=16`. `best_quality` trains the seven families of its
default portfolio (CatBoost, FastAI, LightGBM, PyTorch MLP, Random Forest, XGBoost,
Extra Trees); AutoGluon's foundation-model entries, such as TabPFN and TabICL, are not
in it.

Over 131 datasets it scores 0.8464 against 0.8427 (81 ahead, 43 behind, 7 tied;
Wilcoxon, a single test, p = 6e-5). Most of that sits outside the figures: on the
108 datasets they use the gain is +0.0012 (p = 0.016), on the other 23, twenty that
TabFM refuses for their classes or features and three every model finds trivial,
+0.0159 (p = 4e-5,
`plant-species-leaves-shape` +0.093, `kr-vs-k` +0.084).

The budget is soft in this configuration: 117 of 131 datasets ran over their 1200 s,
42 by more than 10 % and 9 by more than double (worst 3966 s), and the run took 54.8 h
against the 43.7 h the budget implies; the published run never ran more than 17 s
(1.4 %) over. Extra time does not explain the gain. Split by whether the re-run kept
within 10 % of its budget, the datasets that overran gain less in both populations:
-0.0010 against +0.0021 on the 108, +0.0057 against +0.0214 on the 23. The split is
on the re-run's own time, though, and same wall time is not the same compute: Ray
keeps more cores busy, and FastAI, the dropped argument and Ray changed together, so
the gain cannot be split among them.

ECE shows no detectable change (0.0391 to 0.0398, p = 0.6); Brier improves slightly
(0.206 to 0.203, raw p = 7e-4). Ray failed once: at dataset 87 its workers did not
start, no model trained, and the run stopped; resumed from the checkpoint, that
dataset completed normally. The published files are kept as
`results/autogluon_sec_300*.joblib.bak-no-fastai`; the re-run replaces them.

## TabNet was on the CPU the whole time

ResNet and FT-Transformer use `_get_device()`, which selects MPS. TabNet is built
through pytorch-tabnet, whose `define_device("auto")` is:

```python
if device_name == "auto":
    if torch.cuda.is_available():
        return "cuda"
    else:
        return "cpu"
```

MPS is not in it. On Apple silicon "auto" silently means CPU, and no warning is
emitted. Forcing `device_name="mps"` measured **3.9-4.3x faster** with no cost in
score: over 5 seeds per device, `abalone-3class` 0.5398 ± 0.0763 on CPU against
0.5733 ± 0.0245 on MPS, `volcanoes-a3` 0.8243 ± 0.0060 against 0.8140 ± 0.0265.

A single-seed comparison first looked alarming (0.6252 CPU vs 0.4670 MPS), but
CPU's own five-seed range on that dataset is [0.4165, 0.6414]. Device comparisons on
a stochastic model need distributions, not one run each. These runs predate the
batch-size fix described above; the next section re-measures the seed noise after it.

Small data reverses the result. Below 1024 training rows the batches are tens of
rows, MPS dispatch overhead outweighs the arithmetic, and CPU wins:
`autoUniv-au6-1000` (1000 x 40) takes 2 098 s on CPU with the four inner folds in
parallel, against 9 994 s for `autoUniv-au1-1000` (1000 x 20) on MPS with folds in
sequence. The wrapper picks the device by split size.

## TabNet's seed noise is as large as its split noise

On the pipeline as it now runs, each outer fold's final TabNet model was refit from
its stored parameters under 5 seeds; the seed sets both the early-stopping split and
the initialisation (`scripts/tabnet_seed_grid.py`, `results/tabnet_seed_grid.csv`).

| dataset | sd across seeds, within a fold | sd across folds, at one seed | sd across seeds of the 4-fold mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| `abalone-3class` | 0.050 | 0.043 | 0.029 |
| `volcanoes-a3` | 0.012 | 0.011 | 0.006 |

The seed moves a fold as far as the split does, and the benchmark's own number for a
dataset, the mean of its four folds, moves by 0.029 on `abalone-3class` from the seed
alone. `abalone-3class` trains on MPS, which is not deterministic: even seed 0 misses
the stored run by up to 0.043, where `volcanoes-a3`, on CPU, reproduces it exactly.

These are spreads on one dataset, and they do not compare with the gaps between
models' means over 108 or 131 datasets. The noise of such a mean is smaller by a
factor that was not measured: up to the square root of the dataset count if datasets
were independent and equally noisy, and they are not. Only TabNet's seed noise was
measured. What the grid does show is that on any one dataset a gap of a few
hundredths between two models can be the seed. Nothing in the single-number-per-model
format shows it.

An earlier version of this section quoted 0.4165 to 0.6414 across 5 seeds on
`abalone-3class` (sd 0.076). That was measured before TabNet's batch-size fix, in a
setup that was not recorded.

## SVC's 96 hours were memory thrash, not compute

SVC originally recorded 96.3 h, the second most expensive model in the benchmark. Re-run
with identical scoring code on an otherwise idle machine it took **2.41 h**, and
`arcene` went from 22 886.9 s to 9.8 s — a factor of 2300 on one dataset.

The difference was not the algorithm. The original run shared the machine with
processes that drove it into swap. The binding resource in this benchmark is RAM,
not cores: 24 GB, and a single TabFM dataset uncapped allocated 13.55 GB.

A separate real defect: libsvm's SMO solver is unbounded by default and never
terminates on some (config, fold) pairs — workers observed still spinning after
14 h 08 m. `max_iter=2_000_000` bounds it and is verified non-binding: scores are
byte-identical on all comparable datasets.

## A month of stolen cores, invisible from inside

Partway through the ResNet run its rate drifted from 1.31x to 1.58x of historical
over several days. The obvious reading was that the remaining datasets were more
expensive. That was wrong.

Ten orphaned VSCode helper processes, each burning 90-125 % CPU with an RSS under
200 KB, had been running for 33 days and were taking roughly ten of the machine's
fourteen cores. Load average was 82. After killing them the rate returned to 1.15x,
better than the run had ever managed.

The run itself had no way to see this. Every in-process signal — its own CPU
percentage, its memory, its progress per dataset — looked normal. Wall-clock per
dataset was the only affected number, and it is exactly the number a benchmark
publishes.

## An upgrade that moved one model and not another

TabPFN-3.5 needs `tabpfn` 9.0.0; the benchmark had been running 8.2.0. Upgrading a
library that holds every foundation-model number in the report is not free, so the
question was what it changes.

The checkpoint filenames for v2, v2.5, v2.6 and v3 are byte-identical between the
two releases — the weights do not move. Re-running one dataset before and after the
upgrade settled the rest: TabPFN-3 on `wholesale-channel` reproduced its stored
scores exactly, 0.963776 / 0.951391 / 0.925232 / 0.926493, to 0.0e+00.

TabPFN 2.6 did not. On `teaching-assistant-evaluation` it moved by up to 0.033, and
GridSearch picked a different ensemble size, because 9.0.0 scales `n_estimators` for
feature coverage where 8.2.0 did not. Same weights, different inference path.

So TabPFN 2.6 was dropped rather than republished on numbers the current code cannot
reproduce. Its checkpoint is kept on disk under a `.bak-` name; 236 h of compute is
not worth deleting to save 7 MB. The model was eleventh of fifteen and the most
expensive foundation model in the benchmark, so nothing in the conclusions rested
on it.

The general point: pinning a library version is not the same as pinning behaviour.
The weights file was identical and the results still moved.

## Which headline gaps the data actually supports

Every ranking in this benchmark is a list of means, and most gaps between neighbours
are under 0.002. The means alone cannot say which of these gaps are real. The
figures now test them: Friedman as an omnibus, then Wilcoxon signed-rank on every
pair with Holm correction, over the 108 datasets every model scores. One dataset is
one observation; the four folds of a dataset share their data and would inflate the
sample fourfold.

The family is 18 models, so 153 pairs. The three GridSearch baselines from
`compare_baseline_models.py` are left out of it: they are the same learners as their
tuned entries, pairs between the two versions are not comparisons this report makes,
and carrying them would widen the family to 210 pairs and weaken every verdict.

Nemenyi's critical distance is not used. It compares average ranks, and a model's
average rank moves when an unrelated model joins the comparison, so the verdict on a
pair depends on company it never met. Wilcoxon reads only the pair's own scores.
The Holm multiplier still depends on the family size, which is why the family is
stated rather than assumed.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TabPFN-3 over AutoGluon | +0.0092 | 80 / 108 | 2e-08 | 2e-06 | separable |
| AutoGluon over CatBoost | +0.0126 | 74 / 108 | 2e-05 | 0.001 | separable |
| AutoGluon over MLJAR | +0.0123 | 80 / 108 | 3e-07 | 2e-05 | separable |
| MLJAR over CatBoost | +0.0004 | 49 / 108 | 0.31 | 1 | not separable |
| TabFM over TabPFN-3 | +0.0039 | 74 / 108 | 1e-04 | 0.006 | separable |

| TabPFN-3.5 over TabPFN-3 | +0.0037 | 72 / 108 | 2e-04 | 0.007 | separable |

The AutoML rows replace the one that used to lead this table, MLJAR over TabFM at
+0.0295 — ROC AUC set against PR AUC, as the section above explains. On one metric
the weakest foundation model beats AutoGluon on 80 of 108 datasets, all ten
foundation-versus-AutoML pairs separate (the largest Holm p is 2e-6), and AutoGluon
separates from CatBoost too. Against the published AutoGluon run it did not while
CatBoost started from uniform probabilities (Holm p = 0.08); CatBoost's re-run from
the prior moved its mean by 0.0003 and took AutoGluon's wins over it from 71 to 74 of
108 (Holm p = 0.003). The AutoGluon re-run keeps the 74 and widens the gap.

Gaps of 0.004 to 0.007, small enough to read as noise in a table of means, are
consistent enough across datasets to survive correction for 153 comparisons.
CatBoost over Random Forest is the marginal case: separable at Holm p = 0.04 on the
106 datasets of the previous version, not at 0.08 on these 108 with the corrected
AutoML scores in the family, nor at 0.13 after CatBoost's re-run from the prior. A
verdict that one revision of the population flips was never more than borderline.

Two rows show the correction doing opposite things to near-identical evidence.
TabPFN-3.5 over TabPFN-3 is +0.0037 on 72 datasets and survives; TabPFN-3.5-fast
over TabPFN-3 is +0.0020 on 67 and does not. The gap between the two verdicts is
not a difference in kind, it is where 0.05 happens to fall.

The TabFM row is worth reading twice. TabPFN-3.5 has the same mean to within 0.0001
but wins only 38 of the 108 head-to-head — TabFM wins more often, TabPFN-3.5 wins
by more when it does. Means and win counts answer different questions, and neither
alone is the ranking.

The critical-difference figure draws a bar only where every pair inside it is
inseparable. The top bar holds TabFM, TabPFN-3.5, TabICL and TabPFN-3.5-fast;
TabPFN-3 shares the next one with TabICL and TabPFN-3.5-fast. No bar joins a
foundation model to anything outside the family. AutoGluon shares no bar at all,
and CatBoost and MLJAR sit inside the gradient-booster group.

PR AUC is the mean over the four outer folds, as everywhere else in this report.
Brier and ECE pool the folds first: a 300-row fold spread over 15 bins is mostly
binning noise, and averaging that per fold inflates ECE by 1.3x to 1.8x. The
ordering barely depends on the bins: against 15 equal-width bins, Spearman is 0.97
or higher for 5 to 50 equal-width bins and for 15 equal-mass bins, and the same five
models are on top in every case but 50 bins. Second place is the exception:
AutoGluon holds it at 5 to 15 bins, the two are level at 20 (0.0440 and 0.0442), and
TabICL holds it at 30, 50 and equal-mass.

For the single models this cost nothing to measure: the per-fold probabilities and
labels have been in `results/ckpt/<model>.joblib` all along. The AutoML runners
stored scores only, so both frameworks were re-run to keep theirs, in
`results/<framework>_sec_300_ckpt.joblib`.

## CatBoost started from uniform probabilities

Until the re-run described at the end of this section, every CatBoost number in
this report came from fits that started boosting at uniform probabilities (0.5, or
1/K). That is CatBoost's default for Logloss and
MultiClass; XGBoost 3.2 and LightGBM 4.6 start from the class prior, on binary and
multiclass data alike (LightGBM's docs list its `boost_from_average` for binary and
`multiclassova` only, but 4.6 applies it to softmax multiclass too). It matters only
when a fit stays close to its start, and the tuning makes that common: Optuna
maximises PR AUC, which ignores probability scale, so it is free to choose a
tiny `learning_rate * n_estimators`.

In that run the mean ECE of 0.0848 is a tail. CatBoost's median is 0.0498, the lowest of the
boosters (XGBoost 0.0549, LightGBM 0.0510). On the 29 of 108 datasets where the
median fold has `learning_rate * n_estimators` below 5, its ECE is 0.155 and it is
underconfident, by 0.119 on average: on `led7digit` (10 classes, learning rate
0.002) the top class gets a mean probability of 0.29 and is right 74 % of the
time. On the other 79 it averages 0.059 and is level with XGBoost (p = 0.44) and
LightGBM (p = 0.67). XGBoost and LightGBM end up in the same regime about as often
(34 and 44 datasets), but staying near the prior costs them far less: ECE 0.080
and 0.078.

The fix is in `CatBoostNativeWrapper`: each fit starts from the training fold's
log class prior, passed as a `Pool` baseline, and prediction adds the same
baseline. On Logloss this is `boost_from_average=True` to 1e-8; CatBoost 1.2.10
refuses that option for MultiClass, which covers 56 of the 108 datasets.

The start's own effect comes from refitting every outer fold's final model with the
hyperparameters the uniform-start run chose, once from each start
(`scripts/catboost_start_effect.py`, `results/catboost_start_effect.csv`; the
uniform arm reproduces the stored predictions exactly on all 108). On the 78
imbalanced datasets Brier improves on 51 (p = 0.0005), the mean absolute confidence
gap falls from 0.050 to 0.029 and ECE from 0.078 to 0.060. On the 30 nearly balanced
ones (KL(prior || uniform) below 0.01, up to about 56/44 on binary data), where the
prior is close to the uniform start, nothing changes on average (p = 0.72), though
single datasets move, since any change in the start changes the trees: Brier worsens
by up to 0.026 and improves by at most 0.005. PR AUC does not move
(p = 0.53), and ECE over all 108 goes from 0.0848 to 0.0720. Among the
small-budget datasets the gap goes from -0.099 to -0.006 on the 21 imbalanced ones
and stays at -0.17 on the 8 balanced ones: the start removes the offset, and what
is left is undertraining. On the 14 datasets where all three boosters got a small
budget it takes CatBoost's gap from -0.096 to -0.025, against -0.038 for XGBoost and
-0.034 for LightGBM, so the start accounts for CatBoost's excess. On the 8 balanced
small-budget datasets, where it cannot help, the tuning gave XGBoost and LightGBM
larger budgets (median `learning_rate * n_estimators` 6.2 and 4.3 against 1.4), and
their gap is -0.086 and -0.034 against -0.172. With learning rate, trees, depth and
L2 matched, CatBoost from the prior keeps pace with both in log loss
([demo notebook](https://github.com/firefly1248/demo-notebooks/blob/main/notebooks/catboost_boost_from_average.ipynb)).

An earlier version of this section credited the fix with `led7digit`'s ECE falling
from 0.451 to 0.267. That was a full nested CV, in which the tuning also chose
different hyperparameters; `led7digit`'s prior is nearly uniform, and with the same
hyperparameters the start moves it from 0.451 to 0.437. The CatBoost re-run on all
131 datasets re-tunes too, so its before-and-after is the start and the tuning
change together. Over the 108 calibration datasets mean ECE goes from 0.0848 to
0.0685 (0.0720 with the old hyperparameters), the mean absolute confidence gap from
0.059 to 0.039 (p = 4e-4) and Brier from 0.250 to 0.239 (p = 0.022); PR AUC does not
move (p = 0.32). On the 78 imbalanced datasets the re-tuning adds nothing to the
start: the gap is 0.029 with the old hyperparameters and with the new. On the 30
nearly balanced ones, where the start cannot help, the re-tuned run's gap is 0.067
against 0.082, which the test does not separate (p = 0.15). The median fold's `learning_rate * n_estimators` is
below 5 on 29 of 108 datasets in both runs (8 to 6 balanced, 21 to 23 imbalanced):
the re-tuning did not end the undertraining a ranking metric permits.

The re-run took 116.4 h against 73.7 h, and the start is not the cost. Refitting
outer fold 0 of 14 datasets with the same parameters from each start gives a time
ratio of 1.00 (0.91 to 1.26; the same fit timed twice varies by 0.79 to 1.62). The
extra hours come from what the re-tuning chose on a few datasets: on
`multiple-features` (depth 8 to 10 against 5 to 8) the fold-0 parameters fit 15.5
times slower, 3.1x from depth alone and 2.7x from tree count alone, and
`plant-species-leaves-shape` took 52.1 h against 25.2. Over all 131 datasets the
chosen depth went up in a third of folds and down in a third
(`scripts/catboost_retune_effect.py`, `scripts/catboost_fit_time.py`).

`scripts/catboost_calibration.py` reproduces the uniform-start diagnostics above and
writes them per dataset to `results/catboost_calibration.csv`;
`scripts/catboost_start_effect.py` writes the per-dataset comparison of the two
starts, from which the demo notebook computes the group figures; every fold's
chosen parameters are in `results/best_params.csv`, the uniform-start run under
`catboost_uniform_start`.

## Post-hoc calibration does not buy the gap, and it is not free

The obvious objection to the table above is that calibration is a solved problem:
fit a one-dimensional map on held-out predictions and the cheap model catches up.
It does not. Both repairs are cross-fitted over the model's own four outer
folds — each fold is corrected by a map fitted on the other three, so the
calibrator never fits on the labels it is scored against.

**Isotonic regression** is the non-parametric ceiling: one-vs-rest, then
renormalised. **Venn-ABERS** (Vovk et al. 2015) is built from the same isotonic fits,
applied the same way, but refits with each
test point inserted once as a positive and once as a negative, and reports
`p1 / (1 - p0 + p1)`, the paper's log-loss merge; its square-loss merge moves no
figure here by more than 6e-5. On binary problems applying it to both columns and
renormalising equals the canonical one-column method, up to the float rounding in
the model's own probabilities; on multiclass the one-vs-rest renormalisation is a
heuristic that voids the method's validity guarantee.

The implementation was checked against the paper's definition, refitting isotonic
regression once per test point, and against three public implementations. It
matches the definition exactly, ties and out-of-range scores included, and
`ip200/venn-abers` (which MAPIE vendors) to 3e-16. The other two differ only where
they have defects of their own: `ptocca/VennABERS` looks up the wrong knot when a
test score equals a calibration score or exceeds the largest one, and
`fated/venn-abers-predictor` never merges tied scores, so its answer depends on how
ties happen to be sorted. Refitting with sklearn is not a clean reference on real
data either: `IsotonicRegression` merges scores closer than its dtype's
resolution, 1e-6 for float32. That same tolerance was tying scores inside our own
isotonic baseline; it now fits in float64, which moved its columns by at most 0.001.

Cross-fitting over stored predictions makes one approximation. Each fold's
calibrator is fitted on predictions from the other three folds' models, which
were themselves trained on the fold being corrected: its labels never enter the
fit directly, but they shape the models whose scores do. That affects both
repairs alike, so it does not bias the comparison between them, but it is why no
coverage guarantee is claimed for Venn-ABERS here.

Three things come out of it.

*The repair is real but small where it matters.* Random Forest gains the most
(0.0747 to 0.0463, a 38 % cut), then SGD, HistGradientBoosting, TabNet and
CatBoost, at 30-36 %. TabFM and AutoGluon gain least, because they had least to give
back.

*It does not close the gap.* Nothing classical reaches the **untreated** TabFM at
0.0367, and neither AutoML framework does: AutoGluon's best repair is 0.0393,
MLJAR's 0.0397. The nearest classical model afterwards is SVC at 0.0436, and repaired
CatBoost lands at 0.0480, still worse than raw TabPFN-3 at 0.0443. "Just calibrate the
gradient booster" does not produce a foundation model's probabilities.

Counting how many models clear that 0.0367 line exactly is not worth doing: the two
TabPFN-3.5 variants sit within 0.0004 of it after isotonic, and two changes that
moved no raw figure — a numpy upgrade and the float64 fix above — have each moved
these repaired figures in the fourth decimal. The gap that carries
the claim is the 0.0069 between the best repaired classical model and the untreated
foundation floor, not the tie at the line.

*The ceiling costs ranking.* Isotonic loses PR AUC on all eighteen, from -0.0120
(AutoGluon) to -0.0322 (SGD). CatBoost gives up 0.0213, fifteen times its 0.0014 lead
over Random Forest. The loss is ties, not
reordering: on the binary datasets isotonic maps whole score intervals onto one
plateau and keeps a median 8 % of a fold's distinct positive-class scores, so
positives and negatives end up sharing a value.

*Venn-ABERS halves the ranking cost, and wins on binary data.* On the binary
datasets, where it is the method as published, it keeps a median 31 % of a fold's
distinct positive-class scores against isotonic's 8 %. Over all 108 datasets it gives
back PR AUC accordingly: -0.0101 from raw on average against -0.0184, better than
isotonic on all eighteen models and on 107 of 108 datasets (Wilcoxon over datasets,
averaged across models, p = 1e-18).

On calibration the answer splits by task. On the 52 binary datasets Venn-ABERS beats
isotonic on Brier for all eighteen models and 39 of the 52 datasets (p = 9e-5), and
is level on ECE (p = 0.6). On the 56 multiclass datasets, where it runs one class
against the rest and is renormalised, it loses on both: ECE on all eighteen models
(p = 1e-6), Brier on fifteen (p = 0.004). Pooled, the two cancel on Brier and
leave an ECE deficit that belongs to the multiclass heuristic, not to the method.
None of this moves the gap: the best classical ECE after Venn-ABERS is SVC at 0.0502,
further from the untreated 0.0367 than after isotonic. The per-dataset table and these
tests are `scripts/calibration_repairs.py` and `results/calibration_repairs.csv`.

So the calibration ordering is not an artefact of leaving the classical models
unrepaired. It survives repair, and the repair that closes most of it costs more
ranking than the ranking differences this benchmark is able to detect.

## Tuning on log loss buys the calibration that repair could not

The three boosters were re-tuned with one change: Optuna selects on log loss instead
of PR AUC, with the same spaces, trials, folds and seeds (`xgboost_logloss`,
`catboost_logloss`, `lgbm_logloss`; `scripts/variant_effects.py`,
`results/variant_effects.csv`). Calibration is over the 108 datasets of the
calibration table, PR AUC over all 131. Holm runs over a family of four chosen for
these notes, the three log-loss variants and
[Extra Trees](#extra-trees-splits-cost-lightgbm-not-sklearns-forest), not over the
all-models family used elsewhere:

| model | ECE | Brier | mean \|gap\| | PR AUC | Holm p | tuning hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| XGBoost | 0.0627 → 0.0409 | 0.239 → 0.225 | 0.035 → 0.015 | 0.8282 → 0.8257 | 0.08 | 7.0\* → 4.1 |
| CatBoost | 0.0685 → 0.0378 | 0.239 → 0.217 | 0.039 → 0.012 | 0.8342 → 0.8341 | 0.38 | 116.4 → 73.0 |
| LightGBM | 0.0674 → 0.0402 | 0.244 → 0.223 | 0.040 → 0.017 | 0.8306 → 0.8278 | 0.004 | 10.4\* → 4.9 |

\* August runs, not comparable; see below. Mean |gap| is the absolute difference between
mean confidence and the observed hit rate, per dataset.

All nine calibration changes are significant (raw p < 1e-4, which survives a
correction for nine). CatBoost tuned on log loss reaches an ECE of 0.0378, between
TabFM's 0.0367 and AutoGluon's 0.0398 in the calibration table (untested against either), where the best post-hoc repair took it only to 0.0480, with no
measurable change in PR AUC. XGBoost gives up 0.0025, which the correction does not
separate; LightGBM gives up 0.0028 and is behind on 84 of 131 datasets (Holm
p = 0.004). CatBoost's tuning took 73.0 h against 116.4 h, both runs from October.
The XGBoost and LightGBM baselines date from August under other load (see
[A month of stolen cores](#a-month-of-stolen-cores-invisible-from-inside)), so their
hours do not compare cleanly.

A proper scoring rule is meant to reward calibrated probabilities, so the direction is
no surprise; the price is the finding. The chosen parameters suggest where it comes
from, over the same 108 datasets (432 folds per model). Folds with
`learning_rate * n_estimators` below 5, the undertraining the CatBoost section found a
ranking metric permits, fall from 37 % to 22 % for XGBoost and from 29 % to 16 % for
CatBoost. For LightGBM that share does not move (39 % to 40 %), but
`class_weight="balanced"`, which reweights the classes and shifts every probability,
is chosen in 23 % of folds instead of 35 %. These are candidates, not a tested
mechanism.

## Ensembles over the stored predictions never help

Every model stores per-fold probability matrices, so combining them costs arithmetic
rather than compute. `scripts/ensembles.py` tries every combination of two or more
of nine models (the five foundation models, LightGBM, LightGBM-linear, XGBoost,
CatBoost) with probability, logit and rank averaging: 1506 blends on the 111 datasets
all nine score, in `results/ensembles.csv`. The best single model is TabFM at 0.8634,
with TabPFN-3.5 at 0.8632.

| ensemble | combiner | mean | vs best single | win rate | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TabFM + TabPFN-3.5 | logit | 0.8648 | **+0.0015** | 49.5 % | 0.04 |
| TabFM + TabPFN-3 + TabPFN-3.5 | logit | 0.8644 | +0.0010 | 46.0 % | 0.31 |
| TabFM + TabICL + TabPFN-3.5 | logit | 0.8643 | +0.0009 | 48.6 % | 0.29 |
| all five foundation models | logit | 0.8641 | +0.0007 | 45.0 % | 0.54 |
| TabFM + TabICL + TabPFN-3, the previous best | logit | 0.8636 | +0.0002 | 40.5 % | 0.61 |
| best blend with a booster (4 foundation + XGBoost) | logit | 0.8636 | +0.0002 | 46.8 % | 0.79 |
| best blend with CatBoost (5 foundation + CatBoost) | logit | 0.8636 | +0.0002 | 48.6 % | 0.84 |

The best blend gains 0.0015 and wins on half the datasets. Its p of 0.04 is the
best of 1506 tries; Holm-adjusted over them it is 1. Of the 1368 blends that do
separate from TabFM at raw p < 0.05, 1367 are worse. Only 43 blends beat it on the
mean at all, 9 of them with a booster in, none by more than 0.0002.

Two specifics. **A booster never helps a foundation blend**, which refutes the
"always co-train a cheap classical baseline" advice independently of the
blind-spot table, itself a scoring bug. And logit averaging is the best combiner
for 25 of the 26 foundation-only sets; with a booster in the mix, probability
averaging wins a third of the time. Rank averaging is never best. CatBoost joined
the pool only after its prior-start re-run, because a logit blend weighs members by
confidence and the uniform-start CatBoost was underconfident; an earlier version of
this table put the uniform-start CatBoost in the foundation trio at -0.0006. Re-run,
it changes nothing either.

## Extra Trees splits cost LightGBM, not sklearn's forest

`lgbm_extra` is LightGBM with `extra_trees=True`: one random threshold per feature at
each split instead of the best one, with LightGBM's search space, sampler and seed.
Over 131 datasets it is worse: 0.8123 against 0.8306, 54 ahead and 72 behind (Holm
p = 0.005 over the family of four tuning variants, the three log-loss ones and this).
The typical dataset barely moves (median -0.0003); the mean is pulled down by a tail.
It loses more than 0.05 on 21 datasets and gains that much on 2, and loses more than
0.02 on 33 against 11 gains. The 21 are six rule-generated datasets (`nursery-4class`
-0.37, `kr-vs-k` -0.23, `monks3` -0.23, `hayes-roth` -0.21, `car-evaluation`,
`monks1`), five synthetic sets (four `autoUniv` and `artificial-characters`), the
`hill-valley` pair, and eight ordinary tabular sets such as `turkiye-student`,
`thyroid-allbp` and `wine-quality-5class`. `results/lgbm_extra_by_dataset.csv` lists
every dataset with its PR AUC and Brier difference and the share of features with at
most 5 distinct values (the reader's cutoff; the encoders below use 8).

It does not help on small data either: 12 of the 21 large losses are in the middle
third of datasets by rows and 6 in the largest, and the smallest third (n = 44) shows
no consistent gain (median +0.0009, mean -0.006 with `hayes-roth` in it, 23 ahead, 20
behind). Over the 108 calibration datasets Brier gets worse (0.244 to 0.266, raw
p = 0.013); ECE shows no detectable change.

Extra Trees keep exact TreeSHAP: in LightGBM 4.6.0, `pred_contrib`, which
`shap.TreeExplainer` also calls, sums to the raw score within 1e-14. Linear trees have
no TreeSHAP: LightGBM raises on that call.

Five follow-ups, from `scripts/extra_trees_controls.py`. The three tests over all 131
datasets share one Holm family; the two checks on subsets chosen for their loss are
reported without a test. Brier and ECE are over the 108 calibration datasets, raw p.

**The loss is not search noise.** `lgbm_seed1` is LightGBM re-tuned with another TPE
seed. The August run was on numpy 1.26; checked by hand, the current code reproduces
its seed-0 scores on `hayes-roth` to 12 digits, so the seed is the only effective
change. It lands on 0.8309 against 0.8306, 57 ahead and 69 behind (Holm p = 0.27).
The per-dataset difference has a standard deviation of 0.011 and exceeds 0.05 on 2
datasets, against 0.054 and 23 for `lgbm_extra`; on the 21 datasets `lgbm_extra`
loses, the seed moves LightGBM by -0.002 on average and never by more than 0.015.
Against this one control seed `lgbm_extra` loses 0.019 (56 ahead, 73 behind,
Holm p = 0.003), and Brier again (raw p = 0.006).

**The random threshold skips cuts that few-valued features need.** Measured on
LightGBM 4.6.0 with one feature and one target (the probe is in the script): with
`extra_trees`, a feature with levels 1, 2, 3 is only ever cut at 1.5, levels 1 to 4
only at 1.5 or 2.5, and a two-level feature coded 1/2 is never split; coded 0/1 it is.
The top cut of a feature is never drawn, and the empty bin below 1 wastes a draw. The
few-valued features of the losers are coded from 1. Refitting the tuned models on the
full data, `lgbm_extra` never splits 17 of `thyroid-allbp`'s 20 two-level features,
4 of `statlog-australian-credit`'s 4, 4 of `flare`'s 5 and both of each `monks` set,
all features plain LightGBM splits; on `kr-vs-k`, `hayes-roth`, `car-evaluation` and
`turkiye-student` every feature is split, but with 3-8 levels each is missing its top
cut. The same draw explains the continuous losers: it covers the feature's whole range
rather than the node's, so one tree of up to 256 leaves on one continuous feature
grows 4-5 leaves with `extra_trees` against 114 without, and `hill-valley` and
`artificial-characters` are the datasets where 2000 trees recover the most.

On the six rule-generated datasets, features with at most 8 levels were re-encoded.
One-hot makes `extra_trees` a no-op: every column is 0/1, and `lgbm_onehot` and
`lgbm_extra_onehot` are identical on all six (mean 0.956 against 0.769 for
`lgbm_extra`). But one-hot costs plain LightGBM 0.012 on these six (0.968; `kr-vs-k`
0.863 to 0.810), so the cheaper fix is to leave `extra_trees` off. LightGBM's own
categorical splits, at their defaults, cost plain LightGBM more (`lgbm_cat` 0.850;
`kr-vs-k` 0.774): relative to `lgbm_cat`, `lgbm_extra_cat` repairs `nursery-4class`,
`car-evaluation` and `kr-vs-k`, keeps the `monks` losses, and both collapse
`hayes-roth` to chance (0.36): with 120-160 rows and no feature above 4 levels, no
category group reaches the default `min_data_per_group` of 100.

**More trees close part of the gap on continuous features, none on the six.** With
the cap raised from 500 to 2000 on the 21 losers, picked on their 500-tree gap, the
tuning chose a median of 1078 trees for LightGBM and 1408 for `lgbm_extra` (85-88 %
of folds above the old cap). LightGBM did not move on average (-0.001), `lgbm_extra`
gained 0.008, and 16 of 21 gaps narrowed, by 0.037 on `artificial-characters`, 0.026
on `saheart`, 0.023 and 0.019 on the `hill-valley` pair (where LightGBM itself gained
0.021 and 0.014) and 0.019 on `autoUniv-au7-700`; the six rule-generated gaps changed
by 0.01 or less. Three of those closures exceed the 0.015 the seed ever moved
LightGBM on a loser; `lgbm_extra`'s own seed noise was not measured.

**sklearn's Extra Trees do not lose.** `ExtraTreesClassifier` with the Random Forest
search space and encoding (sklearn's default `bootstrap=False` stays) beats Random
Forest: 0.8365 against 0.8320 over 131 datasets, 92 ahead and 36 behind (Holm
p = 3e-7), median +0.002, two losses beyond 0.05 and one gain of 0.051; Brier (0.241
both) and ECE (0.075 to 0.081, raw p = 0.40) show no detectable change over the 108.
The Random Forest checkpoint is from August; re-run by hand on `hayes-roth` and
`wine`, the current environment reproduces its scores and chosen parameters exactly.
On the six rule-generated datasets it ties Random Forest within 0.002 except
`kr-vs-k` (-0.022). That puts it level with CatBoost (+0.002, 71 ahead, 57 behind, raw
p = 0.08, outside any family), outside the published roster and figures. It has no
such hole: sklearn draws the threshold uniformly inside the node's range, so every
cut stays reachable. It tuned in 2.8 h; the 7.1 h of the Random Forest run are from
August on a loaded machine ([A month of stolen
cores](#a-month-of-stolen-cores-invisible-from-inside)) and do not compare.

**Tuning time.** `lgbm_seed1` and `lgbm_extra` ran in October, one job at a time, on
the same 131 datasets: 4.75 h against 3.55 h, median 41 s against 29 s per dataset,
so Extra Trees tune about a quarter cheaper. This is the cost of 50 TPE trials, not
of one fit. The 10.4 h of the August LightGBM run are most likely the loaded machine,
not the model.

The usable rule: leave `extra_trees` off in LightGBM, it saves a quarter of the tuning
time and costs 0.018; for random splits use sklearn's `ExtraTreesClassifier`.

## DART buys nothing over tuned LightGBM or XGBoost

`lgbm_dart` is LightGBM with `boosting_type="dart"` and `max_drop=-1` (the default caps
a round at 50 dropped trees, which would flatten `drop_rate`), searched over LightGBM's
space plus `drop_rate` ~ logU[0.01, 0.5] and `skip_drop` ~ U[0, 0.5], so dropout runs in
at least half the rounds. `xgboost_dart` is XGBoost with `booster="dart"`, searched over
XGBoost's space plus `rate_drop` and `skip_drop` on the same ranges, its other DART settings
at their defaults (uniform sampling, tree normalization). Trials, tree cap, learning-rate range
(up to 0.3), folds and seed are each base's (`scripts/dart_controls.py`,
`results/dart_controls.csv`). The analysis was fixed
before the run: one Holm family of four, DART against its August base and against the
[seed control](#extra-trees-splits-cost-lightgbm-not-sklearns-forest), for LightGBM and
for XGBoost. `xgboost_dart` departs from the plan three times, each decided on time, not on
scores. The plan left it to a timing check; before the run it was queued on every dataset
instead (2026-10-18). During the run both `plant-species` datasets (100 classes) were skipped
when their searches were projected past the 12 h limit (2026-10-28), so its pairs have 129
datasets, and `multiple-features` hit that limit, which the plan scored as missing, and resumed
its stored trials with no limit.

| comparison | PR AUC | ahead / behind | raw p | Holm p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `lgbm_dart` vs LightGBM | 0.8306 → 0.8274 | 38 / 88 | 2e-5 | 7e-5 |
| `lgbm_dart` vs `lgbm_seed1` | 0.8309 → 0.8274 | 54 / 73 | 0.095 | 0.19 |
| `xgboost_dart` vs XGBoost | 0.8293 → 0.8268 | 42 / 82 | 3e-4 | 9e-4 |
| `xgboost_dart` vs `xgboost_seed1` | 0.8282 → 0.8268 | 50 / 75 | 0.098 | 0.19 |

"DART beats tuning" needed a significant gain against both bases, a mean gap larger than the
seed control's own shift and a larger tail. Both DARTs are behind both bases, and the Holm
family leaves only the comparisons with the August runs significant. What follows was added
after those results and is not pre-registered: intervals against pooled controls
(`scripts/dart_pooled_controls.py`, `results/dart_pooled_controls.csv`; 90 % bootstrap
interval over datasets; the controls are `lgbm`, `lgbm_seed1` and `lgbm_rerun` for LightGBM,
`xgboost` and `xgboost_seed1` for XGBoost, all with DART's 50 trials), with the margin 0.001
set after seeing them. LightGBM's DART is 0.0033 behind the pooled controls, [-0.0068,
-0.0008], 47 datasets ahead and 79 behind; XGBoost's 0.0020, [-0.0032, -0.0008], 47 and 78.
The upper bound is below zero against every single control, `lgbm_trials100` (twice DART's
budget) included, and below -0.0008 against the pooled ones, so a mean gain of 0.001 is
excluded at one-sided 95 % for both libraries. The medians are -0.0003 and -0.0002: a small
shortfall on most datasets plus a few large ones. The gap to the seed control alone is as
large (LightGBM -0.0035 [-0.0070, -0.0010], XGBoost -0.0015 [-0.0027, -0.0002]); its Wilcoxon
p (0.095, 0.098) stays high because the medians sit near zero. Past 0.02, LightGBM's DART
gains on 4 datasets and loses on 5 against the August run; against the seed control it loses on
6, three of them (`appendicitis`, `colon32`, `teaching-assistant-evaluation`) where the seed
control had itself gained 0.035 to 0.051. XGBoost's DART gains on 1 and loses on 4 against its
seed control, as often as the seed alone moves XGBoost (`xgboost_seed1`, sd 0.009).

DART's own sampler seed was run for LightGBM only (`lgbm_dart_seed1`, the same search with
sampler seed + 1); XGBoost's DART has one seed. The second seed differs from the first by
+0.0017 [-0.0008, +0.0053], all of it `pendigits`: without it -0.0000 [-0.0013, +0.0013],
against the base's seed shift of +0.0003 (`lgbm_seed1` against `lgbm`). The mean of the two
DART seeds is 0.0024 behind the three controls, [-0.0043, -0.0008], 43 datasets ahead and 84
behind, and 0.0016 behind, [-0.0027, -0.0005], without `pendigits`. For XGBoost, `xgboost_seed1`
is itself 0.0011 below `xgboost` [-0.0024, +0.0002], so part of its DART's gap to the August
run may be seed. So: at 50 trials, 500 trees and a learning rate up to 0.3, DART does not beat
tuned LightGBM or XGBoost; a mean gain of 0.001 is excluded, and the loss is about 0.002
with a median near 0.0002.

Much of the stored LightGBM DART's loss is one fold: without `pendigits` the gaps to `lgbm`
and `lgbm_seed1` are -0.0016 and -0.0018 against -0.0033 and -0.0035. On the fourth outer fold
of `pendigits` the final DART model, refit at the learning-rate bound of 0.3, predicts class 8
for 91 % of rows and scores 0.101, chance for 10 classes, where its other folds and LightGBM
score 0.999. The second DART seed scores 1.000 on that fold, so the expected loss is nearer
0.002 than 0.0033.

That fold is DART's, not the search's (`scripts/dart_pendigits.py`,
`results/dart_pendigits.csv`). The refit reproduces the stored predictions (0.101) and
collapses on its own training rows too (0.101), so it is a degenerate model, not
overfitting. The same parameters score 0.9985-0.9992 on all four inner folds, 0.9996-0.9997
under model seeds 1-3, and 0.9996 under `drop_seed` 1, but collapse again under
`drop_seed` 2 and 3 (0.112 and 0.098). With these parameters the drop pattern decides whether
the model survives: 3 of the 7 patterns tried on the full training rows collapse, a share
that overstates the rate because the first pattern was found by its collapse. LightGBM
draws that pattern from `drop_seed` and the iteration count alone, not from the data, so the
inner folds replayed the final fit's pattern on three quarters of the rows and survived; they
could not screen it out. The parameters sit near the weak-dropout corner (`drop_rate` 0.033,
`skip_drop` 0.44), so weak dropout did not prevent it here.

Refitting every stored best-parameter set under other LightGBM seeds
(`scripts/dart_refit_seeds.py`, `results/dart_refit_seeds.csv`; 524 outer folds, `lgbm` and
`lgbm_dart`, random_state 0 to 3, the search not repeated) puts the gap to `lgbm` at -0.0033
under seed 0, the stored fit, and -0.0022, -0.0022 and -0.0018 under seeds 1 to 3. Seed 0
reproduces 1046 of the 1048 stored fold scores to 1e-9; the other two, `pendigits` fold 3 and
`walking-activity` fold 2, differ by 0.002 and 0.001, probably from thread order, and
`pendigits` still collapses. A fold's score moves by a median of 0.0016 (LightGBM) and 0.0020
(DART) over the four seeds, 0.0097 and 0.0129 at the 90th percentile. Folds more than 0.1 below
the `lgbm` fold: 3, 0, 1 and 1 under seeds 0 to 3; `appendicitis`, `colon32` and `pendigits`
only under seed 0, `habermans-survival` fold 2 under seeds 2 and 3.

Few of these folds are collapses. Folds more than 0.1 below the same fold of the base,
out of 524: 3 for LightGBM's DART and 2 for the second seed, against 1 for `lgbm_seed1`
below `lgbm`, 2 for `lgbm` below `lgbm_seed1`, and none between `lgbm` and `lgbm_rerun`;
XGBoost's DART has 3 of 516 (`appendicitis`, `ozone-onehr` twice) against 1 for `xgboost_seed1`.
Two runs of the same base differ that much too (`colon32` -0.235 and `planning-relax`
-0.186 between `lgbm` and `lgbm_seed1`). Only `pendigits` is chance-level: one fold of 524
in LightGBM's DART under seed 0, none under seed 1 and none in XGBoost's.

Calibration is where DART costs, LightGBM's more. Over the 108 calibration datasets
LightGBM's DART raises ECE from 0.067 to 0.106, Brier from 0.244 to 0.276 and the mean
|gap| from 0.040 to 0.082 (raw p < 1e-5 for each); the rises are heavy-tailed, with medians
of 0.013 (ECE, up on 71), 0.010 (Brier) and 0.013 (|gap|). XGBoost's raises ECE from 0.063 to 0.074 (raw
p 0.005; from 0.067 against the seed control, p 0.015; median rise 0.002, up on 70) and Brier
from 0.239 to 0.253 (p 2e-5), and leaves the |gap| where it was (0.035 and 0.040, p 0.74).

Selected on log loss, LightGBM's DART calibrates close to LightGBM. In a follow-up outside the
family, run after these results with one seed, the same search selected on log loss
(`lgbm_dart_logloss`, same space and budget) is close to LightGBM selected the same way on ECE
(0.042 against 0.040, mean gap +0.002 [-0.002, +0.007], raw p 0.70; a rise of up to 0.007 is
not excluded) and is slightly worse on
Brier (0.227 against 0.223: the mean gap +0.004 [+0.001, +0.008] excludes zero, while the
median is +0.0003 and the signed-rank p 0.12), with PR AUC unchanged (0.8277 against 0.8278,
p 0.44). That search also uses less dropout (46 % of folds in the weak-dropout corner
against 38 %) at a higher learning rate (median 0.114 against 0.092), so selecting less DART
may be part of what log loss does. Why DART calibrates worse under PR AUC is not identified.
XGBoost's DART was not selected on log loss.

LightGBM's search leaned toward weak dropout, XGBoost's did not. The weak-dropout corner (drop
rate < 0.02 or `skip_drop` > 0.45) holds 26 % of the search space; 38 % of LightGBM's DART
folds sit in it (median drop rate 0.040 against the prior's 0.071), 30 % of XGBoost's (0.066).
LightGBM's DART chose a median learning rate of 0.092 against LightGBM's 0.027, XGBoost's 0.055
against 0.031, and the tree cap bound about as often (over 450 trees in 12 % of LightGBM DART's
folds against 16 % and 14 % for its two bases, in 14 % of XGBoost DART's against 12 % and 9 %).

The learning-rate cap of 0.3 binds LightGBM's DART more than its base: it chose a rate above
0.2 in 21 % of folds against 7.6 % for `lgbm` (XGBoost's 7.9 % against 4.5 %). Lifting the cap to 1.0
(`lgbm_wide` and `lgbm_dart_wide`, otherwise the same search; 31 datasets, the 30 of the split
study below plus `pendigits`, which was added because it had collapsed, so not a random
sample) does not help DART. Its median rate goes to 0.173 with 33 % of folds above 0.3 (the
base's 0.050 and 18 %), and it scores 0.0076 below its cap-0.3 self, [-0.0179, -0.0011]. Against
`lgbm_wide` it is 0.0131 behind, [-0.0298, +0.0002], but two datasets carry that (`pendigits`
and `statlog-landsat-satellite`, one chance-level fold each, the second 0.294); without them
it is 0.0005 behind, [-0.0051, +0.0046], where a gain of 0.001 is not excluded, and DART is
still 0.0024 below its cap-0.3 self, [-0.0046, -0.0005]. So on these datasets the cap is not what
hides a DART gain; the arm was not run on the other 100.

Other outer splits (`scripts/dart_split_seeds.py`, `results/dart_split_seeds.csv`). On the 30
datasets of `scripts/split_seeds.py` under outer split seeds 0 to 4, with `lgbm_seed1` rerun on
each split, DART's mean gap to `lgbm` is +0.0010, +0.0018, -0.0008, -0.0012 and -0.0004 (raw
Wilcoxon p 0.10 to 0.87, not combined). The per-dataset gap averaged over the five splits is
+0.0001 against `lgbm`, [-0.0015, +0.0019], 10 datasets ahead and 19 behind, and -0.0002
against the mean of `lgbm` and `lgbm_seed1`, [-0.0013, +0.0011]. Both upper bounds are below the
margin of 0.002, set before the runs of splits 1 to 4, the first by 0.0001; neither is below
0.001. The 30 datasets are also kinder to DART than the 131 (split 0: +0.0010 against
-0.0033), so this shows that the sign changes with the split on a fixed small set, with a
split-to-split sd of 0.0013 in the mean gap, not that DART ties.

XGBoost's DART is slow, and slower the more classes a dataset has. These are the recorded times
of whole searches on a shared machine, not of one fit at fixed parameters. With parallel outer
folds and pauses removed, LightGBM's DART tuned in 9.27 h against 3.95 h for `lgbm_seed1`
re-timed the same way, 2.35x; XGBoost's took 101.8 h on its 129 datasets against 2.36 h for
`xgboost_seed1`, 43x as a ratio of the sums, a lower bound since the two skipped datasets
were projected to be the slowest. Per dataset, DART's time over its seed control's, median
[quartiles] (the 10+ band also misses the two skipped datasets):

| classes | datasets (LightGBM / XGBoost) | LightGBM DART | XGBoost DART |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 56 / 56 | 1.2x [0.7, 1.8] | 5.8x [4.7, 15] |
| 3-4 | 26 / 26 | 1.9x [1.1, 3.2] | 14x [8.4, 29] |
| 5-9 | 26 / 26 | 1.9x [1.0, 6.1] | 29x [22, 40] |
| 10+ | 23 / 21 | 2.0x [1.2, 3.2] | 62x [34, 85] |

The bands also differ in size (median rows 694, 1072, 1966 and 3842), but the classes hold
apart from it: log ratio = -1.13 + 0.79 log classes + 0.41 log rows (R2 0.74), and with the
features and the trees DART chose added, 0.70 log classes, 0.45 log rows, 0.17 log features
and 0.99 log trees (R2 0.85). That is an association, not a cause. `multiple-features` took
16.8 h against 0.17 h, 12 h of it in the segment cut at the limit.

The code is consistent with this, though the weights of its terms were not timed. XGBoost's
DART (`Dart::PredictBatchImpl` in `src/gbm/gbtree.cc`, 3.2.0) rebuilds the prediction every
round from every tree not dropped, one tree at a time, whatever `rate_drop` and `skip_drop`
are; plain boosting adds each new tree to a running prediction. For each tree the rebuild
zeroes a rows x classes buffer and refills every row's features, so its cost grows with the
trees, and through that buffer and those features with the classes and the features.
LightGBM's DART (`src/boosting/dart.hpp`, 4.6.0) only subtracts and re-adds the trees it drops,
none in skipped rounds; its stored ratios follow the rows (Spearman 0.61), not the chosen drop
rate (0.08).

## Fifteen datasets are duplicates

UCI++ reuses the same underlying data in different configurations, and 15 of the
146 are such variants. They used to be listed in each figure notebook and dropped
after the fact, so they were computed and then discarded — 6 of the 7 duplicate
`volcanoes` variants cost ResNet over 2 h each, and about 10 % of the benchmark's compute went
to datasets no figure reads.

The list now lives in `config.DUPLICATE_DATASETS` and the runners skip it, so that
compute is not spent again. The scores already on disk are kept and the aggregates
still span all 146, so nothing published changed; a model added from here on covers
131. Duplicates also inflated the sample in the significance tests, which is the
other reason to drop them before the statistics rather than after.

## The pairs the timeout could not measure — now measured

A timed-out (dataset, model) pair records `time = NaN`, so the hours it burned never
enter the cost figures. Each was re-run with the cap raised. Every answer was worth
the machine time.

**CatBoost on `plant-species-leaves-shape`** (100 classes, 1600 rows, 64 features)
needed **25.16 h** from the uniform start, and **52.11 h** re-run from the prior,
where the re-tuning settled on costlier trees. It had recorded NaN twice before —
14 h with the timeout defeated, then a clean 12 h cut — so the number simply did not
exist. What it buys:

| model | PR AUC | time |
|---|---|---|
| TabICL | 0.8998 | 0.34 h |
| TabPFN-3 | 0.8987 | 0.60 h |
| ResNet | 0.8250 | 1.57 h |
| SVC | 0.7620 | **0.01 h** |
| **CatBoost** | **0.7253** | **52.11 h** |
| Random Forest | 0.7203 | 0.17 h |
| LightGBM | 0.6665 | 0.55 h |

CatBoost places fifth here. SVC scores higher in 24 seconds — **7800x cheaper for
+0.04 PR AUC**. TabICL scores +0.17 higher for 1/150th of the cost. This one dataset is
nearly half of CatBoost's 116.4 h benchmark cost, and it is the single clearest case
in the benchmark of compute buying nothing.

The failure mode is many-class data: CatBoost trains one-vs-all across 100 classes
inside a 50-trial nested search. Nothing about the data is hard — every other model
finishes in under two hours.

**ResNet on `multiple-features`** (2000 x 649) needs **10.40 h** and scores 0.9987.
Above 500 features `_train_rtdl_on_device` falls back from MPS to CPU. Historically
1.20 h at 20 trials; 2.48x more fits predicts 2.98 h on the GPU, so the measured
10.40 h puts the **cost of losing MPS at 3.49x** — close to the 3.5x estimated from
the TabNet device experiment, and enough, multiplied by the trial change, to push a
one-hour dataset past a twelve-hour cap.

**TabNet on `letter` and `tamilnadu-electricity`** (both 10 000 rows) needs **18.12 h**
and **16.19 h**. At a twelve-hour cap and again at twenty they recorded NaN; `letter`
was 36 fits of 804 short the second time. What the 18.12 h buys is 0.9841, between
Random Forest (0.9820, 0.06 h) and SVC (0.9882, 0.04 h), while TabPFN-3.5 takes the
dataset at 0.9988 in 0.49 h. On `tamilnadu-electricity` twelve models reach a perfect
1.0000, Random Forest in 3.6 minutes against TabNet's 16.19 h — **270x** for the same
score.

ResNet and TabNet now cover all 146 datasets, and CatBoost all 131 it runs on.
